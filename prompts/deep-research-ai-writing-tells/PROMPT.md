# Deep Research Prompt: AI Writing Tells — and How to Eliminate Them

**Expert lens:** You are a computational linguist and writing coach with deep expertise in stylometrics, AI text generation patterns, and human voice preservation. You have studied how large language models produce text structurally and rhetorically, and you work with writers to help them identify and remove AI-like patterns from their own output.

**Research question:** What are the most reliable linguistic, structural, and stylistic patterns that signal AI-generated text — and what concrete strategies can a human writer use to audit and eliminate these patterns from their own work?

**Scope constraints:**
- Focus on current LLM output patterns (GPT-4 and later, Claude, Gemini — 2023–2026)
- Scope to written prose: essays, newsletter posts, LinkedIn content, analytical writing — not code, transcripts, or creative fiction
- Evidence standard: prioritise NLP research, writing craft literature, and AI detection tool documentation over opinion pieces; flag where claims are anecdotal vs empirically grounded
- This is about *human writers avoiding AI-like patterns*, not about detecting AI for moderation or academic integrity purposes

---

## Phase 1 — Landscape scan

Establish what's known:
- What does the research say about how LLMs generate text differently from humans? (At the token level, sentence level, structural level)
- What signals do AI detection tools (Originality.ai, GPTZero, Turnitin, etc.) actually use? What have their developers said publicly about what they look for?
- What patterns have linguists, editors, and writing researchers identified as reliably AI-associated?
- How have these patterns shifted as LLMs have improved? What used to be a tell that no longer is?

## Phase 2 — Taxonomy of AI tells

Build a comprehensive, categorised list of AI writing patterns. Cover each category with specific examples of what the pattern looks like in practice and why LLMs default to it:

**Lexical patterns**
- Overused words and phrases (e.g. "delve", "crucial", "it's worth noting", "in the realm of")
- Hedging and filler language
- Abstract nominalisation favoured over concrete verbs
- Superlative and intensifier inflation

**Structural patterns**
- Paragraph and essay structure (does AI default to specific shapes?)
- Transition sentence conventions
- List and bullet point overuse
- Intro/conclusion formulae

**Rhetorical patterns**
- Announcing structure before delivering it ("In this essay, I will...")
- False balance and both-sidesing
- Over-explanation of the obvious
- Conclusions that restate rather than resolve

**Tonal patterns**
- Performed enthusiasm and affirmation ("Great question!", "Absolutely")
- Synthetic authority (confident assertions without grounding)
- Emotional flatness — competent but inert
- Voice averaging — sanding down the idiosyncratic

**Cognitive patterns**
- Surface-level synthesis that lists rather than connects
- Missing genuine point of view or conviction
- Avoidance of specific, personal, or verifiable detail
- Absence of surprise, contradiction, or earned tension

## Phase 3 — Detection and calibration

- What methods do skilled human readers use to identify AI text — consciously or not?
- What makes a piece of writing *feel* like AI even when no single tell is obvious? (Cumulative effect, rhythm, something harder to name)
- Are there patterns that vary by domain — does AI analytical writing have different tells from AI narrative writing?
- What patterns are hardest to eliminate, and why?

## Phase 4 — Elimination strategies

Practical, actionable techniques for writers:
- Audit frameworks: how to read your own draft looking for AI-like patterns
- Rewriting techniques: how to transform AI-adjacent sentences into human ones (with before/after examples where possible)
- Voice preservation when using AI as a drafting tool: what habits protect idiosyncrasy?
- Which patterns are easiest to fix (find-and-replace level) vs which require deeper structural revision?

## Phase 5 — Implications for analytical / essay writing

Specifically for a writer producing analytical essays, newsletter posts, and LinkedIn content:
- Which tells are most common in analytical writing specifically?
- What does distinctly *human* analytical writing look like — what are its markers?
- What's the practical checklist before publishing?

## Source priority

**Prioritise:**
- NLP and computational linguistics research (ACL, EMNLP, arXiv)
- AI detection tool documentation and developer commentary (GPTZero, Originality.ai, Turnitin blog)
- Writing craft publications (The Atlantic, Columbia Journalism Review, Nieman Lab on AI and writing)
- Named researchers or editors who have written specifically about AI stylistics

**Avoid:**
- SEO listicles ("10 signs your content is AI-generated")
- Marketing agency commentary
- Uncited opinion pieces without specific examples

---

## Quality checks before finalising

- Every identified pattern must include a concrete example — not just a description
- Distinguish between empirically documented patterns and expert observation/anecdote
- Taxonomy must be mutually exclusive where possible — don't double-count the same tell under different labels
- Elimination strategies must be actionable, not vague ("write more authentically" is not an answer)
- Flag where the evidence is contested or where patterns are changing as models improve
